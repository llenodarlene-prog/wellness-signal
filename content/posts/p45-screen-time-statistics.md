# Screen Time Statistics: What US Teen Surveys Actually Show

Screen time headlines often blur together phone use, total device time, social media and time online. The numbers sound comparable, but they measure different things. Four hours of screen time isn't four hours on TikTok, and being online "almost constantly" doesn't tell you how many hours someone spent.

The clearest US figure comes from the CDC. From July 2021 to December 2023, 50.4% of US teens aged 12 to 17 had 4 or more hours of daily screen time, not counting schoolwork. The definition and dates are central to that finding.

Here are the key screen time statistics and what they can and can't tell you.

## Teen Screen Time Statistics at a Glance

| Measure | Figure | Who and when |
|---|---|---|
| 4+ hours of daily screen time | 50.4% | Ages 12 to 17, July 2021 to December 2023 (CDC) |
| Ages 12 to 14 with 4+ hours | 45.6% | Same CDC survey |
| Ages 15 to 17 with 4+ hours | 55.0% | Same CDC survey |
| Use the internet daily | 97% | Ages 13 to 17, 2025 (Pew) |
| Online almost constantly | About 4 in 10 | Same Pew survey |
| Say they spend too much time on social media | 45% | Ages 13 to 17, fall 2024 (Pew) |

These rows come from different surveys with different questions and age ranges, so they can't be combined.

## Older Teens Spend More Time on Screens

In the CDC survey, 55.0% of teens aged 15 to 17 had 4 or more hours a day, compared with 45.6% of those aged 12 to 14. That's a 9.4 percentage point gap between the two groups. It doesn't tell you how many extra hours each year of age adds.

Screens cover schoolwork, messaging, entertainment and keeping in touch. This survey can't assign every hour to one purpose.

## Screen Time and Mental Health

Among teens with 4 or more hours of daily screen time, the CDC found that 27.1% had anxiety symptoms and 25.9% had depression symptoms in the past 2 weeks.

That doesn't prove screens caused those symptoms. Mental health can also shape how much time people spend on devices, and other factors can affect both. "Teens with more screen time reported more symptoms" is a link. "Four hours of screens causes depression" is a cause-and-effect claim this kind of survey can't support.

## Social Media Isn't the Same as Screen Time

Pew's December 2025 survey of 1,458 teens found that 97% use the internet every day, and about 4 in 10 are online almost constantly.

| Platform | Teens who use it daily |
|---|---|
| YouTube | About 3 in 4 |
| TikTok | 61% |
| Instagram | 55% |
| Snapchat | 46% |
| Facebook | 20% |

Across YouTube, TikTok, Instagram, Snapchat and Facebook, 36% of teens use at least one almost constantly. A teen can check several apps in an hour, so these platform figures can't be added up to estimate total use.

## Teens Think They're Online Too Much

In a separate Pew survey from fall 2024, 45% of teens said they spend too much time on social media. That's up from 27% in 2023 and 36% in 2022.

This measures how teens judge their own use, not hours on a device. The rise may reflect more awareness, changing expectations or real changes in behavior. The survey doesn't show which.

## Screen Time and Physical Activity

The CDC also looked at activity in the same 2021 to 2023 survey. Among teens with 2 hours or less of daily screen time, 70.4% got 60 minutes of physical activity most days. Among those with 4 or more hours, 54.4% did.

Again, that's a link, not a test of what happens when screen time drops.

## Better Questions for Parents

Instead of treating every hour the same, it helps to separate:

- schoolwork from free time
- staying in touch from scrolling
- bedtime phone use, which can cut into sleep
- exposure to upsetting content
- time that replaces physical activity

These numbers show patterns across groups. They can't diagnose one teen or set a single healthy limit for everyone. ## FAQs

### How Many Teens Have 4 or More Hours of Screen Time a Day?

The CDC found that 50.4% of US teens aged 12 to 17 had 4 or more hours of daily screen time outside schoolwork, from July 2021 to December 2023. That's a survey estimate for that period, not a 2026 measurement from device logs.

### Do Older Teens Spend More Time on Screens?

Yes, in the CDC survey. 55.0% of teens aged 15 to 17 had 4 or more hours a day, compared with 45.6% of those aged 12 to 14. That shows how many cross the 4-hour mark, not average hours or why they use screens.

### Does Screen Time Cause Depression?

The CDC data shows a link with recent symptoms, not proof of cause. Mental health, environment and device use can all affect each other. Proving cause and effect needs research designed to test which way the link runs.

### Is Being Online Constantly the Same as Screen Hours?

No. Pew asks teens how often they use the internet, while the CDC asks about hours a day. The questions measure different things, so their percentages shouldn't be combined into one total.
