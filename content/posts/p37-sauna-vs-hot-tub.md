# Sauna vs Hot Tub: 8 Differences in Heat, Recovery, Safety and Upkeep

A sauna and a hot tub both warm you up, but in very different ways. A sauna surrounds you with hot air or infrared heat. A hot tub heats you through water, and the water presses on your body while you sit in it.

That changes how each one feels, how your heart responds, how much upkeep it needs and which research applies. Here's how the sauna vs hot tub choice breaks down.

## Sauna vs Hot Tub at a Glance

| Factor | Sauna | Hot tub |
|---|---|---|
| Heat source | Hot air or infrared | Water |
| Body immersed | No | Yes |
| Water pressure and buoyancy | No | Yes |
| Water chemistry | None | Needs sanitizer and pH control |
| Legionella risk | Not a water-system issue | Needs active control |
| Upkeep | Cleaning, heater care | Water, filters, sanitizer, cleaning |
| Long-term research | Mostly Finnish cohort studies | Smaller, much of it from Japan |

## 1. Both Are Forms of Heat Therapy

A 2021 review describes passive heat therapy as repeated heat exposure, including hot-water immersion and sauna bathing. Both raise skin blood flow and work the heart and blood vessels. How much depends on the temperature, the time and the person.

A 2026 review looked at 45 studies, 17 on bathtub bathing and 28 on sauna use. Bathing studies came mostly from Japan, and sauna studies mostly from Finnish cohorts. The authors noted that the amount and type of evidence differs between the two, so firm long-term conclusions are limited.

## 2. A Sauna Heats You Without Water

A traditional sauna uses hot air. An infrared sauna heats you more directly, at lower air temperatures. Either way, the stress on your body comes from heat, not from water pressure or floating. Sweating is usually more obvious, because hot air surrounds your skin.

Much of the sauna research comes from long-running Finnish studies. Those show links, not proof that a home sauna prevents disease. ## 3. A Hot Tub Adds Immersion

Sitting in hot water changes more than your temperature. The water presses evenly on your body, and buoyancy takes weight off your joints. That makes it a different experience from sitting in dry heat.

It also means more upkeep. Warm, recirculating water needs filtering, sanitizer and pH control.

## 4. Hot Tubs Carry a Water Safety Risk

The CDC says hot tub water should be no hotter than 104°F (40°C). It recommends these levels:

- **Chlorine:** at least 3 parts per million
- **Bromine:** 4 to 8 parts per million
- **pH:** 7.0 to 7.8

The CDC also has Legionella control guidance for hot tubs. It notes that hot tubs keep water in the range where Legionella grows best, 77 to 113°F (25 to 45°C), and create aerosols people can breathe in. Legionella causes Legionnaires' disease. So water care is a health issue, not just a cleanliness preference. A sauna has no direct equivalent.

## 5. Neither Has Proven Recovery Benefits

The evidence isn't strong enough to name a winner. A 2018 review found that passive heat, including sauna and hot-water immersion, can help endurance athletes acclimate to heat when used with specific protocols.

A 2025 analysis pooled 20 randomized trials of hot-water bathing, saunas, hot yoga and local heating. Most heart and blood vessel markers didn't improve. There was a possible drop in systolic blood pressure, but the authors urged caution. So feeling relaxed after training isn't the same as proven recovery. Our [sauna after a workout](/longevity-recovery/sauna-after-workout-benefits/) guide covers the exercise studies.

Warmth can feel soothing on sore muscles, and floating in water can feel good on your joints. Pick based on comfort and how heat fits your training and hydration.

## 6. A Sauna Is Easier to Maintain

A sauna usually has a simpler routine, because there's no water to balance. A hot tub needs regular checks of sanitizer and pH, plus filter care, water changes and upkeep of the pumps and heater. Factor that work into the decision before you buy.

## 7. Running Costs Depend on How You Use Them

There's no single answer. Costs depend on heater power, insulation, climate, your energy price, water volume, target temperature, the cover and how often you use it.

A hot tub often stays heated for long stretches. Many saunas are heated only for each session. That changes the energy pattern even if the heater ratings look similar. To estimate a sauna's power bill, use the sauna electricity calculator in our [infrared vs traditional sauna](/longevity-recovery/infrared-sauna-vs-traditional-sauna/) guide.

## 8. Both Need Safety Care

Too much heat can cause problems in either one, especially if a health condition affects how you handle heat. Hot tubs add water-specific risks, including infection and drowning. Saunas add heat stress and dehydration without the water hazards.

Alcohol makes overheating more likely in both. If you're pregnant, have heart disease, tend to faint or take medicines that affect blood pressure or heat tolerance, ask your doctor first.

## FAQs

### Is a Sauna Healthier Than a Hot Tub?

There's no strong evidence that either is healthier overall. Sauna research includes more long-term observational data, mostly from Finland. Hot-water bathing has its own studies, mostly from Japan. The two are different enough that health claims should match the specific method used.

### Is a Hot Tub Good After Exercise?

Warm water can feel relaxing and may help with heat acclimation in some training plans. It also adds heat stress after a hard session. A 2025 analysis of 20 trials found passive heat didn't improve most heart and blood vessel markers. Hydration and your total training load matter more than soaking after every workout.

### Can a Hot Tub Replace a Sauna?

It can replace a sauna as your relaxation routine, but it isn't the same environment. A hot tub adds buoyancy and water pressure, while a sauna gives you heat without them. Their upkeep and hygiene needs are also very different.

### What Temperature Is Too Hot for a Hot Tub?

The CDC says hot tub water should be no hotter than 104°F (40°C). That's a safety limit, not a target. Your comfort, health and how long you stay in still affect how well you handle the heat below that temperature.
