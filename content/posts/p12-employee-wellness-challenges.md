# 10 Employee Wellness Challenges for Small Teams and How to Measure Participation

A small business does not need a company gym or an expensive software platform to offer voluntary wellness activities. However, a challenge that employees can join is different from a programme that measurably improves health, attendance, or business results.

These ten employee wellness challenges focus on practical formats that can work across small teams and mixed schedules. Each includes a suggested participation measure, an accessibility consideration, and a realistic limit on what the results can show.

## Key Takeaways

- Short, voluntary challenges can be organised around movement, breaks, social connection, and everyday routines without collecting sensitive health data.
- The CDC recommends **150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week** for adults, alongside muscle-strengthening activity on two days. A workplace challenge should not make those guidelines mandatory performance targets.
- KFF's **2025 Employer Health Benefits Survey** found that 56% of smaller firms offering health benefits provided at least one specified wellness programme, compared with 83% of larger firms.
- Randomised workplace wellness research found improvements in some self-reported behaviours but did not demonstrate significant healthcare spending or employment benefits over the studied periods.
- Measure **eligible employees, voluntary participation, and repeat engagement** separately, rather than treating signups as completed activities.
- Privacy, accessibility, and consent should shape the design before selecting rewards, leaderboards, or software.

## Why Wellness Challenges Need Realistic Goals

A wellness challenge is a time-limited activity that invites employees to participate in a defined behaviour or experience. It could involve regular breaks, trying a new movement activity, sharing a healthy meal idea, or participating in a team walk.

The intended outcome must be clear. A four-week walking challenge may show how many employees joined and completed sessions, but it cannot independently demonstrate lower healthcare costs or improved clinical health.

### What Workplace Research Shows

KFF reports that among businesses offering health benefits, 56% of firms with 10–199 workers and 83% of firms with at least 200 workers offered one or more selected wellness programmes in 2025. These [workplace wellness statistics](/work-wellness/workplace-wellness-statistics-access-participation/) describe employer availability, not the proportion of workers who used the services. KFF's 2026 survey, published in October 2026, did not repeat its wellness programme questions, so the 2025 figures remain its latest.

A 2019 randomised trial involving 32,974 employees found improvements in selected self-reported health behaviours after 18 months. It did not find statistically significant differences in clinical measures, healthcare expenditures, or employment outcomes, and a later three-year follow-up reported similar limitations.

The evidence does not mean workplace activities are pointless. It means programmes should describe achievable objectives and evaluate the outcomes they actually measure instead of promising savings or health changes in advance.

## Ten Employee Wellness Challenges at a Glance

| Challenge | Suggested duration | Participation indicator | Important accessibility safeguard |
|---|---|---|---|

| Scheduled break routine | 2 weeks | Breaks self-reported as completed | Do not penalise customer-facing staff |
| Hydration habit awareness | 1–2 weeks | Optional reminder or reflection | Do not set universal fluid intake quotas |
| Guided relaxation sampling | 2 weeks | Sessions tried | Offer quiet, non-meditative alternatives |
| Screen-free lunch option | 1 week | Optional lunch reflections | Respect shift and remote schedules |

## 1. Flexible Movement-Minutes Challenge

Invite employees to record days when they intentionally make time for an activity they choose. Options can include walking, rolling, dancing, adaptive exercise, or another movement appropriate to their circumstances.

### Track Engagement Without Creating a Competition

An employer can measure the number of participants checking in once or more each week, rather than ranking total steps. This supports participation across different ability levels and reduces incentives to exaggerate activity records.

The CDC advises that adults aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity weekly, but its recommendations are public health guidance rather than a target every employee must disclose or achieve. Use flexible personal goals rather than compulsory biometric tracking.

## 2. Protected Breaks Challenge

Employees choose brief opportunities to step away from work tasks when their role and schedule permit. The activity may involve standing, stretching, changing posture, or resting without a screen.

### Make the Schedule Fair Across Roles

A team can record whether employees had a reasonable opportunity to take their scheduled breaks, without demanding proof of exactly what they did. Workers handling customer calls or time-sensitive services may need alternative break windows.

This programme is particularly useful as an operational question: does the workplace permit staff to take breaks already available to them? It should not shift responsibility to workers when staffing or workload prevents those breaks.

## 3. Hydration Awareness Challenge

A hydration challenge can focus on access to drinking water and optional reminders rather than individual consumption quotas. Water needs differ according to health, activity, climate, and other circumstances.

### Measure Access, Not Medical Behaviour

Ask whether team members can reach suitable drinking water during their workday, and invite optional feedback about facilities. Recording litres consumed or requiring a fixed intake target is unnecessary for evaluating workplace access.

This format suits businesses with different working environments, including remote workers and staff at physical sites. It should avoid implying that a specified quantity of water is appropriate for every person.

## 4. Guided Relaxation Sampling Challenge

Offer a selection of brief guided relaxation, breathing, or quiet reflection sessions from reputable sources. Participation can involve trying one session and noting privately whether the format was useful.

### Count Sessions Tried, Not Stress Reduction

An anonymous optional tally of sessions completed is a reasonable engagement indicator. Asking staff to reveal mental health diagnoses, medication, or personal distress is not needed and creates unnecessary privacy concerns.

Some employees may prefer quiet reading, music, or a non-meditative activity. The challenge should allow alternatives and avoid suggesting that a brief exercise substitutes for clinical mental healthcare.

## 5. Screen-Free Lunch Option

Participants may choose one meal break without work email or meetings where their working conditions allow it. The emphasis is on an uninterrupted break rather than a claim that avoiding a screen produces measurable health improvements.

### Evaluate Whether the Break Was Available

The useful indicator is whether staff had an opportunity for an uninterrupted meal period, not whether they reported perfect compliance. Employees may need devices for personal accessibility, childcare, or scheduling reasons.

A screen-free lunch should be optional and never used to criticise individuals whose circumstances differ. Managers may learn more by fixing meeting patterns than by monitoring personal device use.

## 6. Team Walking and Rolling Challenge

A team can organise short voluntary outdoor walks or accessible movement sessions at convenient times. Remote participants can use an equivalent locally suitable activity, and wheelchair users should be able to participate on equal terms.

### Avoid Step-Count Leaderboards

Measure how many participants attended one or more sessions and whether they returned during the programme. Step counts are not directly comparable across different devices, mobility aids, or individual circumstances.

Before scheduling group walks, check physical accessibility, weather, route safety, and whether participation occurs during paid working time. The activity should never become a condition of favourable employee treatment.

## 7. Strength and Mobility Exploration

Provide optional introductions to light resistance, mobility activities, and adapted movement taught through credible resources. The goal is exposure to available movement formats, not athletic testing or body-composition change.

### Offer Adaptations and Professional Guidance

Employees can choose an appropriately modified session and record participation without submitting performance results. Some participants may have health conditions, disabilities, or injuries that make particular movements unsuitable.

The CDC recommends muscle-strengthening activity on at least two days per week for adults. A workplace activity should use that recommendation as general context, while leaving personal exercise decisions to employees and their qualified healthcare professionals when needed.

## 8. Voluntary Recipe and Meal-Sharing Week

Invite employees to share a meal idea, recipe, or food preparation tip without prescribing weight-loss goals. Contributions can include budget meals, culturally meaningful dishes, convenience options, and adaptations for dietary needs.

### Keep Food Discussions Inclusive

Track the number of optional contributions or attendees in an informal recipe exchange. Do not record staff weight, calorie intake, dietary diagnoses, or adherence to a company-preferred eating plan.

The activity can encourage practical conversation, but participation does not establish improved nutrition. Avoid scoring meals as inherently good or bad, and respect allergies and personal dietary requirements.

## 9. Peer Connection Moments

Offer a short optional group activity, such as a lunchtime hobby chat, informal coffee session, or shared-interest meetup. This can be designed for remote and in-person teams without forcing staff to reveal personal experiences.

### Let Employees Choose Their Level of Participation

Measure only aggregate attendance or anonymous preferences for future activities. Employees should be free to decline, participate asynchronously, or attend without speaking about sensitive topics.

A positive experience may encourage future participation, but a single activity cannot demonstrate stronger retention or productivity. Those outcomes require separate measurement and appropriate comparison periods.

## 10. Choose-Your-Own Wellness Week

Let employees choose among several optional activities rather than expecting everyone to complete the same task. A participant might select a brief walk, a protected break, a cooking activity, or a quiet reflection session.

### Use Participation as Feedback for Future Programmes

A simple anonymous survey can ask which options people tried and whether the timing or format was workable. The purpose is to learn what employees can realistically access, not to identify individuals as more or less committed to wellness.

This format is useful for mixed-role teams because it avoids a single standard based on mobility, work schedule, or personal preferences. It also provides a low-cost pilot before committing to [employee wellness software](/work-wellness/employee-wellness-software-small-teams/) or paid providers.

## What Can Small Teams Measure Without Collecting Health Data?

Useful metrics should correspond to the programme's original purpose. For most short challenges, an employer can reasonably track registration, participation, completion, employee feedback, and whether the activities were accessible.

| Measure | Calculation or method | What it can show | What it cannot show |
|---|---|---|---|
| Participation rate | Participants ÷ eligible employees × 100 | Reach of the optional activity | Clinical benefit |
| Repeat-engagement rate | Repeat participants ÷ initial participants × 100 | Continued interest | Long-term habit formation |
| Completion rate | Participants meeting the stated optional activity threshold ÷ joiners × 100 | Programme follow-through | Productivity improvement |
| Accessibility feedback | Anonymous questions about schedule and format | Operational barriers | Individual medical needs |
| Programme cost | Vendor fees + staff time + supplies | Financial inputs | Return on investment alone |

For example, if 12 of 30 eligible employees opt into a programme, the participation rate is **40%**. This is a hypothetical calculation, not a published national average. If eight participants return for another session, repeat engagement among initial joiners is **66.7%**.

### Should Employers Offer Rewards?

KFF's 2025 survey found that **23% of smaller firms** offering the selected wellness programmes used incentives, compared with **46% of larger firms**. The measures apply to firms offering health benefits and the programme categories surveyed, rather than all US workplaces.

Low-pressure recognition may be appropriate, but employers should avoid rewards tied to sensitive health disclosures or compulsory achievement. Laws and benefit-plan rules can apply to wellness programme incentives, so any financial design needs appropriate HR and legal review.

## A Four-Week Pilot for a 20–40-Person Team

A small employer can start with one optional activity and a simple participation method rather than purchasing software immediately. The purpose of the pilot is to identify whether the idea is accessible and attractive enough to repeat.

1. **Week 1:** Ask staff anonymously which activities and times would be workable, and announce the opt-in rules.
2. **Week 2:** Run one chosen challenge with several accessible participation options.
3. **Week 3:** Continue the activity, address scheduling barriers, and avoid public individual performance rankings.
4. **Week 4:** Collect aggregate participation and optional feedback, then decide whether to revise or discontinue the programme.

An employer should decide in advance what information will be collected, who can view it, and when it will be deleted. Personal health data is rarely necessary to evaluate a small workplace activity challenge.

## Employee Wellness Challenges and the Business Case

Workplace wellness activities are often marketed as tools for reducing absence, healthcare expenditure, or staff turnover. The causal evidence behind such claims is substantially more limited than the prevalence of wellness programmes might suggest.

The Song and Baicker randomised trial reported improved selected health behaviours but no statistically significant healthcare cost or employment differences after 18 months. Its three-year follow-up likewise did not find significant differences in clinical markers, health spending, absenteeism, tenure, or job performance.

That does not prevent small employers from offering optional activities for connection, access, or enjoyment. It simply means those goals should be stated and measured on their own terms, rather than supported by unverified promises of financial savings.

## FAQs

### What Are Easy Employee Wellness Challenges for Small Teams?

Low-cost options include voluntary movement sessions, scheduled break reminders, shared recipe ideas, short guided relaxation sessions, and social connection activities. The best option depends on staff schedules and accessibility, while participation should remain optional and avoid collecting unnecessary medical or personal information.

### How Long Should a Workplace Wellness Challenge Last?

A one- to four-week pilot can provide enough time to observe initial signups, repeat participation, and scheduling barriers without creating a permanent employee obligation. There is no single research-backed ideal duration for every activity, so employers should adapt timing to workload and staff feedback.

### What Is a Good Employee Wellness Challenge Participation Rate?

There is no universal participation rate that defines success for all employers. A team should calculate joiners as a share of eligible staff, then compare repeated participation and accessibility feedback against its own baseline rather than borrowing a figure from unrelated programmes or organisations.

### Do Employee Wellness Challenges Reduce Healthcare Costs?

Not necessarily. Randomised studies of broader workplace wellness programmes have observed some improvements in self-reported behaviours without statistically significant healthcare spending reductions over studied periods. A short team challenge should not promise cost savings unless a suitable evaluation demonstrates them for that programme.

### Should Employers Use Step Competitions or Weight-Loss Contests?

Public step rankings can disadvantage staff with mobility limitations or different devices, while weight-loss contests may raise privacy and wellbeing concerns. Voluntary, adaptable participation measures are generally more inclusive and can still show whether employees find an activity practical and engaging.

### Does a Small Company Need Employee Wellness Software?

Not for a simple pilot. Anonymous surveys, a calendar, and aggregated participation counts may be enough to evaluate a short voluntary activity. Software becomes more relevant when an employer needs repeat administration, privacy safeguards, multiple locations, or reliable reporting at scale.

## Conclusion

Small teams can run useful employee wellness challenges without expensive infrastructure, provided that the activities are voluntary, practical, and accessible. The strongest early measure is whether staff can and want to participate, not a speculative improvement in healthcare spending or productivity.

Start with one small pilot, review the evidence and feedback, and adjust the programme around the workforce. The goal is a useful workplace activity, not a leaderboard that rewards the healthiest or most available employees.
