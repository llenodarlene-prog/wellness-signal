# Percentage of Americans Who Exercise Enough: 2024 CDC Data on Activity Guidelines

Many Americans exercise, but how many get enough to meet the federal guidelines? That's a narrower question than whether someone has a gym membership or went for a walk last week. The CDC measures it with a national survey and a clear definition.

In 2024, 47.2% of US adults met the federal guidelines for aerobic activity in their leisure time, according to the CDC's April 2026 report. So the percentage of Americans who exercise enough by that standard is just under half. It isn't the share who exercise at all, the share who lift weights or the share who have a gym membership.

Here's how that number breaks down and how it has changed.

## The Trend From 2020 to 2024

| Survey year | Adults meeting aerobic guidelines | Inactive adults |
|---|---|---|
| 2020 | 47.1% | 27.0% |
| 2022 | 47.3% | 27.2% |
| 2024 | 47.2% | 26.2% |

The CDC says these changes aren't statistically significant. The share meeting the guidelines has barely moved. Calling the drop from 47.3% to 47.2% a "decline" would overstate a tenth of a point.

## What Counts as Meeting the Guidelines

Federal guidelines call for at least one of these each week:

- 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity, such as brisk walking
- 75 minutes of vigorous aerobic activity, such as running
- an equal mix of the two

Adults should also do muscle-strengthening activity on at least 2 days a week. The 47.2% figure covers the aerobic part only, during leisure time.

So you can walk every day and still fall short if the pace or time doesn't reach the threshold. And you can meet the guideline by cycling or swimming without logging many steps. Our [how many steps per day](/fitness/how-many-steps-per-day/) guide explains how steps relate to these targets.

## Younger Adults Are More Likely to Meet Them

| Age | Met aerobic guidelines, 2024 |
|---|---|
| 18 to 34 | 54.0% |
| 65 and older | 38.4% |

The share falls with age. The survey doesn't explain why. Health, disability, work, neighborhood design and access to safe places to exercise can all play a part.

## Men vs Women

In 2024, 52.3% of men and 42.4% of women met the aerobic guidelines. That's a gap of about 10 percentage points. It's a survey result, not a measure of how much either group values exercise, and it doesn't show a cause.

## How This Differs From Gym Statistics

Our [fitness statistics by activity and age](/fitness/fitness-statistics-by-activity-and-age/) cover gym membership and what people do at the gym. Membership doesn't equal meeting the guidelines. The fitness industry can sign up more members while the share of active adults stays flat. More gyms and more paying members don't automatically mean a more active population.

## What a Real Improvement Would Need

To claim a genuine change, you'd need the same survey questions, a clearly defined population and a statistically meaningful shift. It would also help to separate leisure-time exercise from activity at work or getting around, which this measure may not fully capture.

The CDC figures cover civilian adults who don't live in institutions, interviewed through the National Health Interview Survey.

## What Wellness Programs Should Measure

A workplace challenge that counts logged workouts measures participation. It doesn't prove a population got fitter. A good program tracks access, steady attendance, minutes of activity and outcomes separately, because each answers a different question. ## FAQs

### What Percentage of US Adults Meet Exercise Guidelines?

The CDC reported 47.2% for 2024, based on leisure-time aerobic activity in the National Health Interview Survey. That's the share meeting the aerobic guideline, not the share who ever exercise or belong to a gym.

### Are Americans Becoming More Active?

Not clearly. The CDC's figures for 2020, 2022 and 2024 were 47.1%, 47.3% and 47.2% meeting the aerobic guidelines. The agency says these changes aren't statistically significant, so the data doesn't show a real rise or fall.

### Does a Gym Membership Mean Someone Meets the Guidelines?

No. Membership is something you pay for, while meeting the guidelines depends on how long and how hard you exercise. Plenty of people meet them without a gym, and some members rarely go.

### Do These Figures Include Strength Training?

No. The 47.2% figure covers aerobic activity only. Federal guidelines also recommend muscle strengthening on at least 2 days a week, and meeting the aerobic part doesn't mean someone met both.
