# Average Heart Rate by Age: Resting Ranges, Max Heart Rate and What They Mean

Most charts of average heart rate by age mix up three different numbers. Some show resting heart rate. Some estimate the highest rate you'd reach during hard exercise. Others show training zones based on a formula. None of them is a true average for everyone your age.

For adults, the normal resting range is 60 to 100 beats per minute (bpm), according to MedlinePlus from the National Library of Medicine. That range covers adults of all ages, including seniors. Children run higher, and well-trained athletes often run lower.

Here's what each number means and how to read your own.

## Three Different Heart Rate Numbers

| Number | What it describes | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Resting heart rate | Beats per minute while calm and still | Tracking your own trends |
| Estimated maximum | A rough upper limit during hard exercise | Planning workout intensity |
| Target zone | A percentage of your estimated maximum | Setting how hard to train |

None of these is a measured population average. They're reference ranges and estimates.

## Normal Resting Heart Rate by Age

Resting heart rate changes most during childhood. MedlinePlus lists these normal ranges.

| Age | Normal resting heart rate |
|---|---|
| Newborns, 0 to 1 month | 70 to 190 bpm |
| Infants, 1 to 11 months | 80 to 160 bpm |
| Children, 1 to 2 years | 80 to 130 bpm |
| Children, 3 to 4 years | 80 to 120 bpm |
| Children, 5 to 6 years | 75 to 115 bpm |
| Children, 7 to 9 years | 70 to 110 bpm |
| Children 10 and older and adults, including seniors | 60 to 100 bpm |
| Well-trained athletes | 40 to 60 bpm |

Notice that adults share one range from age 10 onward. There's no official resting target for each decade of adult life. Charts that list a precise "ideal" resting rate for every 5-year age band rarely cite measured data.

## Estimated Maximum Heart Rate by Age

Maximum heart rate does fall with age, and that's the number most "by age" charts actually show. It's estimated from a formula, not measured.

The classic formula is 220 minus your age. A 2001 study by Tanaka and colleagues pooled 351 studies with 18,712 people and proposed 208 minus 0.7 times age. They found the classic formula underestimates maximum heart rate in older adults.

| Age | 220 − age | 208 − 0.7 × age |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | 200 bpm | 194 bpm |
| 30 | 190 bpm | 187 bpm |
| 40 | 180 bpm | 180 bpm |
| 50 | 170 bpm | 173 bpm |
| 60 | 160 bpm | 166 bpm |
| 70 | 150 bpm | 159 bpm |

Both are averages for people your age. Your real maximum can be quite a bit higher or lower. Only a supervised exercise test measures it directly. To turn these into training zones, use the [Heart Rate Zone Calculator](/heart-rate-zone-calculator/).

## Why Resting and Workout Rates Differ

Resting heart rate is best measured when you're calm, not right after climbing stairs, drinking coffee or rushing to a meeting. During exercise your rate climbs because working muscles need more oxygen and blood.

That matters for wearables. A watch can show your interval heart rate and a resting estimate on the same screen. Comparing them without noticing the context gives you a misleading story. Watches also calculate resting heart rate in their own ways, which may not match counting your pulse at rest in the morning.

## What Changes Your Resting Heart Rate?

Age is only part of the picture. Fitness, medicines such as beta blockers, pregnancy, dehydration, stress, illness and health conditions can all move your resting rate. Heat does too, which is why your pulse climbs in a sauna, as our [sauna vs steam room](/longevity-recovery/sauna-vs-steam-room-benefits-differences/) guide explains.

Someone who trains often may sit below 60 bpm without any problem. MedlinePlus lists 40 to 60 bpm as normal for well-trained athletes. On the other hand, a reading inside the normal range doesn't rule out a health issue.

## How to Check Your Pulse

1. Sit quietly for a few minutes.
2. Place two fingers on the inside of your wrist, below the thumb.
3. Count the beats for 30 seconds, then double it.
4. Note the time and what you were doing beforehand.

Don't press hard on your neck. If you use a wearable, check its instructions, since many are less accurate during movement. ## When to Pay Attention

Look for patterns rather than single readings. A sudden change that lasts deserves more attention than small day-to-day swings. MedlinePlus notes that resting rates that are continually high or low may need checking.

Get medical help right away if a fast, slow or irregular heartbeat comes with chest pain, fainting, dizziness or shortness of breath, whatever the number on the screen says.

## FAQs

### What Is a Normal Resting Heart Rate for Adults?

MedlinePlus lists 60 to 100 bpm for adults, including seniors. Well-trained athletes are often 40 to 60 bpm. That's a normal range, not a target. Medicines, fitness and health conditions can all shift your number, so context and symptoms matter.

### Is 80 BPM a Normal Resting Heart Rate for a 40-Year-Old?

Yes, 80 bpm falls within the normal adult range of 60 to 100. One reading can't tell you much about heart health, though. Your usual pattern, medicines, fitness and any symptoms matter more than comparing one number to a chart.

### Is 220 Minus Age Accurate?

It's a rough estimate, not a measurement. A 2001 analysis found it underestimates maximum heart rate in older adults and proposed 208 minus 0.7 times age instead. Your real maximum can still differ, and only a supervised exercise test measures it.

### Why Does My Watch Show a Different Resting Rate Each Day?

Sleep, stress, recovery, illness, alcohol, hydration and the watch's own formula all affect the number. Compare readings taken in similar conditions, and focus on changes that last for days or weeks rather than single-day swings.
