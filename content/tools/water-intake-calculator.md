How much water you need each day has no single exact answer, but there is an official reference point. The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine set an Adequate Intake for total water: the amount that healthy people typically consume from drinks and food. This calculator shows that reference, splits it into drinks and food, and converts it to ounces and cups.

Choose a reference group, add any exercise and pick the weather. The calculator explains how those factors change needs without turning a population figure into a personal prescription.

## The Daily Water Reference

The National Academies' 2004 report on water and electrolytes set these Adequate Intakes for adults aged 19 and over:

| Group | Total water a day | About 80% from drinks |
|---|---|---|
| Men | 3.7 L (125 oz) | About 3.0 L (100 oz) |
| Women | 2.7 L (91 oz) | About 2.2 L (73 oz) |

"Total water" includes water in food as well as all beverages, including tea, coffee, milk and juice. Survey data used in the report showed that drinks provide about 80% of total water and food about 20%. Fruits, vegetables and soups add more water than dry foods.

## What an Adequate Intake Means

An Adequate Intake is not a minimum or a target that every person must hit. It reflects what healthy people in the United States typically consume. The panel did not set an upper limit for water and noted that most healthy people meet their daily needs by letting thirst guide them and by drinking with meals.

That is why the calculator shows a reference rather than a precise personal number. Body size, diet, activity, climate and health all influence how much fluid you need on a given day.

## How Exercise and Heat Change Needs

Sweating increases fluid loss, so active days and hot weather raise needs above the reference. Sweat rates vary widely between people and with intensity, temperature, humidity and clothing, which is why the calculator does not add a fixed amount for exercise.

Practical steps for active or hot days include drinking before, during and after longer exercise and paying attention to thirst. The National Academies report notes that needs rise with heat and physical activity, so the reference figure is a floor for comparison on those days rather than a ceiling.

## Who Should Get Personal Advice

Some people need specific fluid guidance from a clinician rather than a general reference. This includes people with heart failure, kidney disease or liver disease, people taking medicines that affect fluid balance, and anyone who has been told to limit fluids. Pregnancy and breastfeeding also change needs.

If you have one of these conditions, follow your clinician's advice even if it differs from the figures here.

## Turning the Reference Into Daily Habits

A reference figure is most useful when it translates into simple routines:

- **Spread drinks through the day** rather than drinking a large amount at once.
- **Include water-rich foods** such as fruit, vegetables and soups.
- **Keep a bottle nearby** during work and exercise.
- **Count other drinks.** Milk, tea and coffee all contribute to total water.

Hydration interacts with other habits too. Poor sleep, long workdays and hot environments can all make it harder to notice thirst. ## FAQs

### How much water should I drink a day?

The National Academies' Adequate Intake for total water is 3.7 liters a day for men and 2.7 liters for women aged 19 and over, including water from food. About 80% typically comes from drinks, which is roughly 3.0 liters for men and 2.2 liters for women. Needs vary with activity, heat and health.

### Does coffee or tea count toward water intake?

Yes. The National Academies' total water figure includes all beverages, including tea, coffee, milk and juice, as well as water in food. Plain water remains a simple, calorie-free choice, but other drinks also contribute to your daily fluid intake.

### Do I need more water when I exercise?

Usually, yes. Sweating increases fluid loss, and losses rise with intensity, heat and humidity. Sweat rates differ widely between people, so the calculator does not add a fixed amount. Drink before, during and after longer sessions and let thirst guide you.

### Can I drink too much water?

It is possible, though uncommon in healthy people. Drinking very large amounts in a short time, especially during long endurance events, can lower blood sodium. Spreading fluids through the day and drinking to thirst helps avoid this. Seek medical advice if you have a condition that affects fluid balance.

### Does the calculator store my answers?

No. It runs entirely in your browser and needs no account. Your choices are not saved, sent to analytics or shared, and refreshing the page resets them.
