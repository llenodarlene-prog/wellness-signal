Walking calories burned depend mostly on three things: how much you weigh, how fast you walk, and whether the ground slopes upward. This calculator uses the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) walking equation to turn those inputs into an estimate of calories, distance and intensity.

Enter your weight, choose a pace and set an incline. The result updates as you change any value, so you can compare a flat stroll with a brisk uphill walk in seconds.

## How the Calculator Works

The ACSM walking equation estimates how much oxygen your body uses while walking. Oxygen use is then converted to calories.

| Step | Formula |
|---|---|
| Oxygen use (mL/kg/min) | 0.1 × speed + 1.8 × speed × incline + 3.5 |
| Calories per minute | Oxygen use × body weight (kg) ÷ 1,000 × 5 |
| Total calories | Calories per minute × minutes walked |
| METs | Oxygen use ÷ 3.5 |

Speed is in meters per minute and incline is a decimal, so a 5% grade is 0.05. The final "3.5" is the resting component, which is why the result is a gross estimate: it includes the energy you would have used sitting still.

### A Worked Example

A 160 lb (72.6 kg) person walking at 3.0 mph on flat ground moves at about 80.5 meters per minute. Oxygen use is 0.1 × 80.5 + 3.5, or about 11.5 mL/kg/min. That equals about 4.2 kcal per minute, or roughly 126 calories in 30 minutes. Adding a 5% incline raises oxygen use to about 18.8 mL/kg/min and the 30-minute estimate to roughly 205 calories.

## Why Incline Matters So Much

The incline term is 1.8 × speed × grade. At a 5% grade it adds 0.09 × speed, which nearly doubles the speed-related part of the equation (0.1 × speed). That is why even a modest slope raises the estimate sharply. On a treadmill, small grade changes are an easy way to raise intensity without walking faster.

The calculator also shows your result on a MET scale. One MET is the energy you use at rest. The CDC classes activity that burns 3 to 5.9 METs as moderate intensity and 6 METs or more as vigorous. It lists walking briskly, at 2.5 mph or faster, as an example of moderate activity.

## How Accurate Is the Estimate?

No formula matches your exact energy use, and this one has known limits. A 2021 study in *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* by Moore and colleagues reported that the ACSM equation is most accurate at speeds of about 1.9 to 3.7 mph. The same study found that newer cadence-based equations were more accurate, and that the ACSM equation was biased by about 0.6 to 0.8 METs in their sample.

In practice, that means the calculator is useful for comparing walks and tracking changes, but it should not be read as a precise measurement. Fitness level, walking efficiency, terrain and footwear all shift real energy use.

### Gross vs Net Calories

Gross calories include your resting energy use. Net calories remove it, showing only the extra energy the walk required. Because the calculator reports gross calories, the figure will be higher than a net estimate for the same walk. Many fitness trackers also show gross totals, though methods vary by device.

## How Walking Fits Weekly Activity Guidelines

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) says adults need 150 minutes of moderate-intensity physical activity a week, such as brisk walking. Five 30-minute walks meet that figure. The calculator can show how a routine like that adds up in calories, but the main benefits of regular walking go well beyond energy use.

Walking also makes a practical starting point for people returning to exercise. Speed and incline can rise gradually as fitness improves. The [fitness statistics by activity and age](/fitness/fitness-statistics-by-activity-and-age/) show how common walking and treadmill use are among US adults.

## Tips for Using the Results

- **Use your current weight.** Calorie estimates scale directly with body weight.
- **Pick the closest pace.** If you walk at 3.2 mph, the 3.0 mph card is a reasonable choice.
- **Compare, don't count precisely.** The estimate is best for comparing walks, not for exact calorie budgeting.
- **Track time and steps too.** Pair this tool with the [Steps to Calories Calculator](/steps-to-calories-calculator/) if your tracker reports steps rather than minutes.

## FAQs

### How many calories does walking burn per mile?

It depends on your weight, pace and incline. For a 160 lb person walking at 3.0 mph on flat ground, the ACSM equation gives roughly 84 calories per mile, including resting energy. Heavier people and uphill walks burn more per mile, so use the calculator for your own figures.

### Does walking faster burn more calories?

Yes, per minute. A faster pace raises oxygen use, so you burn more calories in the same time. Per mile, the difference is smaller, because a faster walker covers each mile in less time. Incline usually has a larger effect than a small increase in speed.

### Is the walking calories burned estimate exact?

No. The ACSM walking equation estimates average energy use, and research has found errors of around 0.6 to 0.8 METs. Individual efficiency, terrain and fitness all change real values. Treat the number as a useful estimate for comparing walks rather than a precise measurement.

### Why does the calculator only go up to 3.5 mph?

The ACSM walking equation is most accurate at about 1.9 to 3.7 mph, according to published validation research. Above that range, many people break into a jog and different equations apply. Keeping the pace cards inside the validated range keeps the estimate more reliable.

### Is my information stored?

No. The calculator runs entirely in your browser. It needs no account, does not save your entries and does not send them to analytics or any other service. Closing or refreshing the page clears everything you entered.
