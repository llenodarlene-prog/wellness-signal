Climbing takes time, so a hike's duration depends on more than distance. This hiking time calculator uses Naismith's rule, a rule of thumb from 1892 that's still widely used: allow an hour for every 3 miles, plus an hour for every 2,000 feet of climbing. You can choose a slower pace for rough ground and add breaks.

## How to Use This Calculator

1. Enter the trail distance and total elevation gain.
2. Choose miles and feet, or kilometers and meters.
3. Pick a pace: Naismith's standard, off-path or relaxed.
4. Add break time, then read your estimate.

## How the Calculator Works

| Part | Naismith's rule |
|---|---|
| Distance | 1 hour per 3 miles (5 km) |
| Climbing | 1 hour per 2,000 feet (600 m) of ascent |
| Off-path pace (Aitken) | 2.5 mph (4 km/h) on surfaces that aren't paths or roads |

A 2023 University of Edinburgh paper notes that the rule works out to about 5 km/h on the flat, plus 10 minutes for every 100 m of ascent.

### A Worked Example

A 6-mile hike with 1,500 feet of climbing, at Naismith's pace, with 30 minutes of breaks:

1. **Distance:** 6 ÷ 3 = 2 hours (1:55:52 using the exact 5 km/h)
2. **Climbing:** 1,500 ÷ 2,000 = 0.76 hours, or **45:43**
3. **Walking time:** **2:41:36**
4. **With breaks:** **3:11:36**

## Its Big Weakness: Descents

Naismith's rule adds no time for going downhill. The 2023 Edinburgh paper calls this a well-known limitation: it doesn't change the predicted speed on descents, however steep. On steep, rocky trails, plan extra time for the way down.

## Limitations

- **It's a planning estimate,** not safety advice. Check weather and daylight, and leave a margin.
- **Fitness and load matter.** A heavy pack, altitude or tired legs slow you down.
- **Terrain varies.** Scrambling, snow, mud and river crossings take longer than any rule predicts.

## FAQs

### How Long Does It Take to Hike a Mile?

On flat, easy trail, Naismith's rule assumes 3 miles an hour, or 20 minutes a mile. Add time for climbing: an extra hour for every 2,000 feet.

### What Is Naismith's Rule?

It's a hiking-time rule from 1892: an hour for every 3 miles on the map, plus an hour for every 2,000 feet of ascent. In metric terms, that's about 5 km per hour plus 10 minutes per 100 m of climb.

### Does Naismith's Rule Account for Going Downhill?

No. It adds no time for descents, which is its best-known weakness. Allow extra time on steep descents.
