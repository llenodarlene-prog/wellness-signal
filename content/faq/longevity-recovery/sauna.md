A sauna session is simple: sit in dry heat, sweat, cool down and drink water. The questions start with the details. How long is too long? How hot should it be? Does any of it burn fat?

These answers draw on guidance from Harvard Health and Cleveland Clinic and on the long-running Finnish cohort studies. Most of the heart research comes from traditional Finnish saunas, so we flag where infrared saunas may differ.

## Time and Temperature

The two numbers people ask about most.

### How long should you stay in a sauna?

Most healthy adults should keep a session to about 15 to 20 minutes. Harvard Health and Cleveland Clinic both use that range. If you're new to saunas, Cleveland Clinic suggests starting with about 5 minutes and adding time over later sessions.

Leave sooner if you feel dizzy, sick or uncomfortable. The time limit is a ceiling, not a target.

### How hot should a sauna be?

A traditional sauna usually runs between 150°F and 195°F (about 65°C to 90°C), with humidity below about 20%. That's Cleveland Clinic's range. Harvard Health puts the top end around 185°F.

Hotter isn't automatically better. Start at the lower end and work up only if you feel fine. In a public sauna, the operator usually sets the heat, so your main control is how long you stay.

### Why does a sauna feel so intense?

Your body works hard to shed heat. Harvard Health says skin temperature climbs to about 104°F within minutes. Your pulse can rise by 30% or more, so your heart pumps close to twice as much blood each minute. Most of that extra blood goes to the skin to help you cool down.

That's why a sauna can feel a bit like light exercise. It's also why people with heart conditions should check with a doctor first.

## How Often

Frequency is where the long-term research gets interesting, and where it's easy to overread.

### How often should you use a sauna?

There's no official target. In a Finnish study of 2,315 middle-aged men followed for about 20 years, men who used a sauna 4 to 7 times a week had lower rates of fatal heart disease and death from any cause than men who went once a week. Most men in the study went 2 or 3 times a week.

That study was observational. It shows a link, not proof that saunas caused the difference. Frequent sauna users may also differ in health and lifestyle. Pick a routine you enjoy and can repeat safely.

### Do longer sauna sessions add more benefit?

Possibly, up to a point. In the same Finnish study, sessions longer than 19 minutes were linked to a lower risk of sudden cardiac death than sessions under 11 minutes. That link didn't hold for death from any cause.

Again, this is association, not cause. It also doesn't override safety advice. Harvard Health still recommends no more than 15 to 20 minutes per session.

## Weight and Hydration

Saunas make you lose weight on the scale. Almost all of it is water.

### Does a sauna burn calories or fat?

Not in any way that matters for weight loss. Cleveland Clinic says there's no clear evidence that a sauna helps you burn fat or lose weight. The drop on the scale after a session is water you sweated out. Once you rehydrate, it comes back.

If weight loss is your goal, regular exercise is the evidence-backed route. The [Walking Calories Burned Calculator](/walking-calories-burned-calculator/) shows what a daily walk adds up to.

### How much water should you drink after a sauna?

Harvard Health suggests 2 to 4 glasses of cool water after each sauna. The average person sweats out about a pint during a short session. Cleveland Clinic advises drinking water before and after too, because dehydration is one of the biggest sauna risks.

Thirst, headache, cramps or confusion are signs you're low on fluids. To see what your daily total looks like, try the [Water Intake Calculator](/water-intake-calculator/).

### Is a sauna after a workout good for recovery?

The evidence is mixed. A 2025 systematic review of 14 studies with 194 participants found inconsistent effects on short-term recovery. Rehydrate after training before you add sauna heat on top, since you'll already have lost fluid. We cover the studies in [sauna after a workout](/longevity-recovery/sauna-after-workout-benefits/).

## Sauna Types

Traditional, infrared and steam all heat you in different ways.

### Is an infrared sauna as hot as a traditional sauna?

No. Infrared saunas usually run between 110°F and 135°F (about 43°C to 57°C), according to Cleveland Clinic. They heat your body more directly with infrared lamps, so the air stays cooler.

Lower air temperature doesn't make them more or less effective. Most long-term heart research studied traditional Finnish saunas, not infrared cabins or blankets. Our [infrared vs traditional sauna](/longevity-recovery/infrared-sauna-vs-traditional-sauna/) guide compares heat, cost and research.

### Is a sauna better than a steam room?

Neither is clearly better. A sauna uses dry heat, while a steam room uses moist heat. Cleveland Clinic says there's no clear sign that dry or wet heat is better. Choose the one you find more comfortable. Our [sauna vs steam room](/longevity-recovery/sauna-vs-steam-room-benefits-differences/) comparison goes through the differences.

## Safety

Saunas are safe for most healthy adults, but not for everyone.

### Who should avoid saunas?

Cleveland Clinic advises against a sauna if you've recently had a heart attack or stroke, or if you've been drinking alcohol. It recommends checking with a clinician first if you:

- are pregnant or trying to get pregnant
- are older than 65 or younger than 16
- have heart disease, severe aortic stenosis, heart failure, or high or low blood pressure
- have certain neurologic conditions, such as epilepsy

Harvard Health adds that people with uncontrolled high blood pressure or heart disease should talk to their doctor first.

### Is it safe to drink alcohol before a sauna?

No. Both Harvard Health and Cleveland Clinic say to avoid alcohol before a sauna. Harvard also warns against it afterward. Alcohol adds to dehydration and makes overheating more likely. Harvard gives the same warning for medicines that affect sweating.

Don't use a sauna when you're ill, either. If you start to feel unwell during a session, get out, cool down gradually and drink water.
