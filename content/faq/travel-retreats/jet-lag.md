Jet lag hits when your body clock is still running on home time after you've landed somewhere else. Your sleep, energy and appetite stay out of step until your internal rhythm catches up with the local clock.

These answers draw on the CDC Yellow Book, the CDC's guide to travel health. To estimate your own recovery time, try the [Jet Lag Calculator](/jet-lag-calculator/).

## Understanding Jet Lag

What causes it and how long it tends to last.

### What causes jet lag?

Your body runs on an internal clock, called a circadian rhythm, that controls when you feel sleepy and alert. Flying across time zones moves the clock on the wall faster than your body can follow. The CDC Yellow Book explains that your rhythms stay anchored to home time for the first few days, then shift gradually toward local time.

### How long does jet lag last?

It depends on how many time zones you cross and which way you fly. The CDC Yellow Book gives average adjustment rates of about 1 hour per day after flying east and about 1.5 hours per day after flying west. So crossing 6 time zones eastward could take about 6 days, and the same trip westward about 4.

### Is jet lag worse flying east or west?

East, for most people. The CDC Yellow Book explains that the body's internal clock runs on a cycle slightly longer than 24 hours, so people naturally drift a little later each night. Flying west asks your clock to go later, which fits that drift. Flying east asks it to go earlier, which works against it.

### What are the symptoms of jet lag?

The CDC Yellow Book lists disturbed sleep, trouble thinking clearly, daytime sleepiness, feeling generally unwell and stomach or digestive upset. Symptoms usually start within 1 to 2 days of travel and ease as your body clock adjusts to local time.

## Before You Fly

Small changes before the trip can shorten the adjustment.

### Can I prepare for jet lag before I travel?

Yes. The CDC Yellow Book suggests shifting your sleep toward your destination's time in the days before you fly. For 2 to 3 days before the trip, move your sleep 1 hour a day: earlier for eastward travel and later for westward travel. That can cut the time it takes to adjust once you arrive.

### Does it help to plan my trip schedule?

It can. The CDC Yellow Book suggests choosing a flight that arrives at a time that lets you get daylight when it helps most. Since adjustment takes several days, try to keep the first days after a long eastward flight lighter where you can.

## On the Trip and After

How to sleep and adjust once you're on the move.

### Should I sleep on the plane?

Sleep loss during travel can make jet lag symptoms worse, the CDC Yellow Book says. The Yellow Book suggests timing meals, sleep and light on the flight to your destination's clock. It also advises staying hydrated, avoiding too much alcohol and avoiding long-acting sedative medicines.

### How does light help with jet lag?

Light is one of the main signals that resets your body clock. The CDC Yellow Book advises getting as much natural light as you can at your destination, and lists timed light exposure among the standard approaches for jet lag disorder. A [Jet Lag Calculator](/jet-lag-calculator/) can help you plan.

### Does melatonin help with jet lag?

The CDC Yellow Book notes that the American Academy of Sleep Medicine's options for jet lag disorder include timed melatonin. Timing matters, and melatonin isn't right for everyone. Talk to a clinician before using it, especially if you take other medicines or have health conditions.

### What can I do to sleep better after I land?

Stay awake through the local day if you can, which builds sleep pressure for the night. The CDC Yellow Book says short naps of 20 to 30 minutes can help you stay alert, but long daytime naps make it harder to sleep at night.
