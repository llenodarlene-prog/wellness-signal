Most new runners do too much, too soon. The fix is usually boring: run a little, walk a little, and repeat it a few times a week. The US Physical Activity Guidelines say injury risk tracks the gap between what you usually do and what you try next, so a slow build matters even when you feel fine.

These answers draw on the US Physical Activity Guidelines, the NHS Couch to 5K plan and published studies on injuries, step rate and shoes. Where the research is weaker than the advice you'll hear online, we say so.

## Getting Started

Start here if you haven't run in a while, or ever.

### How do I start running?

Start with run-walk intervals three times a week, with a rest day between runs. Keep the running parts easy enough that you could still talk. Add a little more running each week.

The NHS Couch to 5K plan works this way. It takes beginners from no running to 30 minutes nonstop over 9 weeks, and you can take longer if you need to. The US Physical Activity Guidelines give the same advice in general form: "start low and go slow," then build how often and how long you exercise before you push the pace.

Not sure how fast "easy" is for you? The [Running Pace Calculator](/running-pace-calculator/) turns a time and distance into a pace you can aim for.

### How often should a beginner run?

Three times a week is a sensible starting point. Couch to 5K uses three runs a week with rest days in between, which gives your legs time to recover. For context, the US guidelines call for at least 75 minutes of vigorous activity a week, such as running, or 150 minutes of moderate activity. A beginner plan can reach that over a couple of months.

If three days leaves you sore, keep the same number of runs but make them shorter. Missing a week is fine. Go back a step rather than jumping ahead.

### Is run-walk training a real way to train?

Yes. Run-walk intervals let you spend more total time moving before you get tired. That's why most beginner plans, including Couch to 5K, start with short runs split by walking breaks.

The walking parts aren't cheating. They keep the effort manageable while your muscles, tendons and bones adjust. Over the weeks, the running stretches get longer and the walks get shorter.

## Building Endurance

Endurance comes from steady, easy running over weeks, not from hard sessions.

### How do I increase my running endurance?

Run more easy minutes each week, slowly. Most of your running should feel comfortable, at a pace where you can talk but not sing. That's the US guidelines' "talk test" for moderate effort.

Add time before you add speed. The US guidelines advise raising duration and frequency first, then intensity. They also note that injury risk tracks how big each jump is compared with what you already do. A 20-minute increase is about 10% for someone running 200 minutes a week, but 50% for someone running 40.

To see what your easy and hard efforts look like in heart rate terms, try the [Heart Rate Zone Calculator](/heart-rate-zone-calculator/).

### Does the 10% rule prevent running injuries?

Not on its own, based on the best trial so far. In a Dutch randomized trial of 532 novice runners, a 13-week plan built on the 10% rule gave the same injury rate as a standard 8-week plan. Injuries hit 20.8% of the graded group and 20.3% of the standard group.

That doesn't make big jumps safe. A Danish study followed 874 novice runners with GPS watches. Those who increased their distance by more than 30% over two weeks seemed more prone to overuse injuries such as runner's knee and shin splints. The authors called the finding exploratory. A practical takeaway is to avoid sudden spikes rather than treat 10% as a magic number.

### How long does it take to build running endurance?

Plan on weeks, not days. Couch to 5K expects about 9 weeks to go from no running to 30 minutes nonstop, and many people take longer. That's normal.

The US guidelines note that older adults may need 2 to 4 weeks to adapt to each new level of activity. Younger adults can usually increase in small amounts every week or two. If a week feels too hard, repeat it before you move on.

## Cadence and Form

Cadence is how many steps you take per minute. It's one of the few form changes with lab evidence behind it.

### What is running cadence?

Running cadence, or step rate, is the number of steps you take per minute. At a set speed, a higher cadence means shorter steps, and a lower cadence means longer ones. Most running watches and apps show it automatically.

There's no single ideal number for everyone. Your height, leg length and pace all change your natural cadence. That's why research tends to test small changes from your own normal rate rather than one universal target.

### How do I increase my running cadence?

Raise it in small steps from your current rate, about 5% to 10%, while keeping the same pace. A metronome app or a playlist at the right beats per minute makes this easier.

In a lab study of 45 recreational runners, a 5% increase cut the energy absorbed at the knee. A 10% increase also reduced load at the hip. The authors concluded that small cadence increases can substantially reduce loading on the hip and knee. The study measured joint loads, not injuries. So it supports trying a small change, but it doesn't prove a higher cadence prevents injuries.

## Shoes and Gear

You don't need much gear to start, but shoes are worth some thought.

### How do I choose running shoes?

Pick the pair that feels most comfortable when you run in it. That's the main practical advice from a 2015 British Journal of Sports Medicine review by Nigg and colleagues. They found no conclusive evidence that impact forces or foot pronation, the old reasons for shoe types, predict injuries.

They proposed a "comfort filter" instead. Runners tend to choose shoes that feel right and let them move naturally. Try a few pairs, jog in them if the store allows, and skip any that pinch or rub. Price and tech labels matter less than fit. Our [best free running apps](/fitness/best-free-running-apps/) comparison covers apps that track how many miles each pair has done.

### Do I need a running app or watch to start?

No. A phone timer is enough for run-walk intervals. Apps help when you want guided plans, GPS distance or a record of your runs. Couch to 5K has its own free app, and many others offer free beginner plans.

A watch adds heart rate and cadence. Those are useful once you're running regularly, but they won't make your first few weeks any easier.
